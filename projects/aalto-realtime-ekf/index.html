Work / Real-time smartphone EKF

Research internship · Aalto University, Dept. of Electrical Engineering & Automation · Jul – Sep 2025

A smartphone as the navigation sensor for a ground robot

An iPhone streams its IMU, GNSS and magnetometer readings to a Raspberry Pi on a DJI RoboMaster S1, where an 8-state Kalman filter estimates pose, velocity and sensor biases in real time. I built the pipeline end to end and recorded 25 runs.

Platform
DJI RoboMaster S1 ground robot with an iPhone and a Raspberry Pi; MATLAB simulation for tuning
My role
Individual Erasmus+ internship project, supervised by Prof. Simo Särkkä with Asst. Prof. Kundan Kumar as advisor: filter design, simulation, real-time implementation, experiments and analysis.
Key results
  • 43.7–48.6 Hz filter output rate logged on the Pi across 25 recorded runs (50 Hz target)
  • 1.77° mean difference from the integrated gyro over three 90° corners
Evidence
hardware · recorded robot data — no ground truth was recorded, so the real runs show real-time operation and internal consistency, not accuracy.

The problem

A phone already carries an IMU, GNSS and a magnetometer. Could it be the whole navigation sensor suite for a small robot, with the filtering done in real time on a Raspberry Pi riding along? A slowly driving ground robot is a hard case for heading: the accelerometer barely sees it, GNSS course is unreliable at low speed, and an indoor magnetometer is often unusable.

What I did

I wrote the phone-to-Pi streaming receiver[1] and an 8-state planar EKF (position, heading, velocity, two accelerometer biases and a gyro bias). Besides GNSS and magnetometer updates, it uses pseudo-measurements from the robot’s motion — no sideways velocity, and zero velocity or turn rate when stationary — which add heading and bias information when the other sources are weak. How much they help depends on how the robot moves and which measurements are available. Covariance updates use the Joseph form[2]. Before touching hardware I tuned the filter in MATLAB against sensor models with bias drift, GNSS multipath and magnetic interference[5].

Result on the robot

Over 25 recorded runs, the Pi logged filter output at 43.7–48.6 Hz against the 50 Hz target[3]. On the run above, the filter follows each 90° turn closely (1.77° mean difference from the integrated gyro over three corners)[4]. It is less steady on the straights: after the second and fourth turns its heading moves by about +28° and −37° while the gyro registers less than 1° of rotation, so the shift comes from the filter’s own heading corrections rather than from the robot turning. That is why the two curves end up as much as 32° apart[7]. Indoors the magnetometer was gated out in about 95 % of samples, which is why the motion constraints matter.

Limitation

There was no motion capture or other ground truth, so no accuracy figure is claimed for the real runs; the checks above are consistency checks against another sensor[6]. The heading corrections on the straights are the clearest open problem: the log does not record which update produced them. Runs were short (up to about a minute) and indoors.

Data path, logged rates, the full run dashboard and the 3-D filter

A second, 9-state filter (3-D position, velocity and attitude, with barometer updates) was aimed at a multirotor and was evaluated in MATLAB simulation only. The repository README also describes ROS/PX4 deployment and ground-truth error analysis that the code and logs do not support; this page relies only on what they show.
