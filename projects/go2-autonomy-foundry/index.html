Work / Quadruped exploration & return-home

Independent project · simulation (Isaac Sim, ROS 2) · Autumn 2026

A simulated quadruped that explores, finds landmarks and returns home

A simulated Unitree Go2 explores an unseen building, maps five coloured flags as 3-D landmarks and walks back to its start. After diagnosing why missions failed, the closed system returned home in 30 of 30 unseen worlds where the inherited one managed 21.

Platform
Unitree Go2 model in Isaac Sim with simulated LiDAR, RGB-D camera and IMU; autonomy stack in ROS 2
My role
Mission and acceptance criteria, system integration, failure diagnosis, pre-registered evaluation protocols, experiments and analysis.
Key results
  • 30/30 vs 21/30 returned home on the same 30 unseen simulated worlds (closed vs inherited system)
  • 31/36 returned home across three unseen simulated building layouts
  • 39/40 returned home in a separate frozen 40-world campaign (95 % CI 0.87–1.00)

In that run it confirmed 5 of 5 flags, did not fall and stopped 0.19 m from its start[14]. This is a single demonstration run. The numbers below come from separate frozen campaigns.

The problem

Every component a legged robot needs for this mission already exists: locomotion, LiDAR-inertial SLAM, terrain mapping, path planning and open-vocabulary detection. The hard part is making them run together, unattended, every time, and measuring that honestly. A stack can pass every component test and still lose missions. Everything here is simulation; no physical robot was used[7].

What I did

I integrated a complete stack (simulated Go2, learned locomotion, GLIM SLAM, an elevation-map terrain pipeline, Nav2, open-vocabulary semantic mapping and a mission executive). Then I treated each lost mission as a fault to diagnose. The two largest losses were not in any single component:

  • Start-up. A middleware reply could be lost while Nav2 was activating, and the executive waited forever. A bounded lifecycle probe (deadlines, retries, back-off) replaced the single query.
  • Return home. The executive sent one return goal and gave up on failure. Nav2’s standard recovery, clearing the costmap, cannot help here: the costmap is rebuilt from the terrain estimate twice a second. The bounded return supervisor adds a stall watchdog, at most three attempts within a fixed time budget, and a traversed-trail prior that frees cells the robot has physically walked through.

Third-party components (GLIM, Nav2, Grounding DINO + SAM 2, the Isaac Lab locomotion lineage) are pinned and unmodified. The evaluation protocols were written down before their data existed: development, validation and test worlds were allocated in advance, and ground truth stays out of the robot’s software by construction and by test.

Main result: the same 30 unseen worlds, before and after

The earlier before/after numbers (12/20 vs 39/40) came from different worlds, so they could not separate the system from the worlds[3]. To control for that, I ran both frozen systems on the same 30 unseen worlds with identical seeds.

The inherited system returned home in 21/30 worlds and the closed system in 30/30. All 9 discordant worlds favour the closed system (exact McNemar p = 0.0039; paired difference +0.30, 95 % CI 0.12–0.48)[9]. Coverage did not change and neither arm fell. The closed system also carries a lower colour threshold, which shows up as false-positive landmarks in 4/30 missions against 0/30.

Does it hold up elsewhere?

An independent frozen campaign. Before the paired study, the closed system was frozen at one commit and run once on 40 other held-out worlds. It returned home in 39/40: 25 on the first attempt and 14 after a supervised retry. The one loss was a fall[1].

Building layouts it was never designed on. All of those worlds share one floor-plan family. I therefore wrote generators for three different topologies (a looped office, a warehouse with shelf aisles and an irregular multi-room plan), froze them, and ran the frozen system once on 12 worlds of each.

It returned home in 31/36 registered missions[10]. That is lower than on the base layout, with more collisions, and the intervals are wide. The test also exposed a defect in my own generator: three worlds spawned the robot within half a metre of a terrain feature, and one of those runs ended in a fall 26 s in. After a geometric fix and replacement by reserve worlds, the geometry-corrected descriptive set returned home in 32/36. The registered 31/36 remains the primary result.

Which recovery does the work?

On 20 further unseen worlds with return-focused missions, a single-shot return succeeded in 13/20 missions, retry with a stall watchdog in 14/20, and retry with the trail prior in 20/20[11]. The trail-prior effect is large, but it misses the pre-registered Holm-corrected threshold (p = 0.062). I therefore report it as indicated, not demonstrated. In doorway round trips, bounded retry fixes controller stalls; clearing the costmap added nothing over a plain resend.

Locomotion

The walking policy is not a new RL algorithm. It continues NVIDIA’s pretrained Isaac Lab Go2 rough-terrain lineage. Evaluating it in the deployed environment showed that the training-time randomisation events (foot friction, mass) had never been applied there, and that mismatch, not the policy, caused most falls. A constrained continuation training run then added posture and contact penalties, more zero-velocity commands and checkpoint averaging.

On 2,832 held-out episodes, falls went from 64 to 8 with the configuration fixed and to 4 with the stabilised policy. Zero-command yaw drift fell from about 50° to about 1° per 30 s[4]. Locomotion is frozen in all mission results.

Semantics

Pooled over the 40 missions, landmark precision was 0.974 and recall 0.95[2]. An evaluator-side funnel shows where recall is lost: every flag that came into view long enough was detected and confirmed, and the 10 missing flags were never in view[13]. The remaining bottleneck is viewpoint coverage, not missed detections.

Limitations

  • Simulation only: one simulator (Isaac Sim), one robot model, and environment families from my own generators. Nothing has been transferred to a physical Go2.
  • Lower reliability on new layouts: 31/36 against 30/30 on the base layout, with more collisions and wide intervals.
  • Residual navigation stalls: controller stalls at doorways and the stationary stretches visible in the video still occur; the supervisor absorbs many of them but does not remove them. A bare navigation regression goal still fails occasionally when phantom lethal cells form around a stationary robot[15].
  • Terrain-edge hazard: the one fall of the 40-world campaign happened next to a 15 cm step edge. The hazard is quantified, not removed.
  • Semantic viewpoints: recall is limited by flags that never come into view.
  • Statistics: 30 pairs and 12 worlds per layout family give wide intervals; the trail-prior mechanism is indicated, not demonstrated.

Status. An IEEE T-RO-style manuscript describing this work is in preparation. It has not been submitted or peer-reviewed, and is available on request.

Architecture, SLAM, negative results and the earlier campaign

The 40-world campaign, mission by mission.

SLAM. The earlier campaigns suggested a small rise in SLAM trajectory error. On identical worlds it does not reproduce meaningfully: 0.07 m vs 0.075 m, a difference of +0.005 m (95 % CI -0.003 to +0.014 m)[12]. No SLAM parameter was changed.

Learned frontier selector. A PPO selector trained in a 2-D surrogate showed no detectable difference from the deterministic heuristic in 10 paired missions, so the heuristic was kept[6].
