Robot control coursework: manipulators in Gazebo and a real TurtleBot

A semester of ROS/C++ robot-control projects: inverse kinematics and Jacobian-level pick-and-place for serial arms in Gazebo, then planning and navigation for a TurtleBot, first in simulation and then on the lab robot.

Platform
Simulated RRP and RRR arms in Gazebo; lab TurtleBot localised by an overhead camera
My role
Projects 1–4 implemented by me. Term project in a three-person team: I designed and implemented the system; teammates contributed in part to navigation and colour detection.
Evidence
simulation · hardware — no success rates or timings were recorded, so none are claimed.

Manipulators

Three projects built serial arms from scratch in ROS and Gazebo; the course set the tasks, and the modelling, kinematics, planners and controllers are mine[1]. They covered closed-form and Jacobian pseudo-inverse inverse kinematics for RRP and RRR arms, a workspace mapper, and — in the last project — velocity-level pick-and-place among obstacles: damped least-squares inverse kinematics, a 3-D Dijkstra planner with obstacle inflation, and potential-field control along the planned path.

Mobile robots

The second half moved to a differential-drive TurtleBot: each task in Gazebo first, then on the lab robot, localised by an overhead camera tracking a fiducial marker. The course provided the robot drivers and the colour-detection node[6].

  • Multi-goal delivery. Odometry with exact arc integration, an A* planner with obstacle inflation, an optimal visiting order based on planned path cost, and Pure Pursuit with obstacle repulsion[3]. On the real robot the camera pose arrived 100–200 ms late, so the turn-rate limit had to be cut to 1.0 rad/s[5].
  • Term project. Explore a 3 m × 3 m workspace, localise coloured identifiers on unknown objects and return within 0.2 m of the start[7]. I designed and built the system — a latency-compensating pose estimator, a coverage planner, A* with smoothing, a dynamic-window local planner and a mission manager — with teammates contributing in part to navigation and colour detection[4].

Limitation

There are no recorded demo scores, logs or videos, so no success rate, accuracy or timing is claimed.
