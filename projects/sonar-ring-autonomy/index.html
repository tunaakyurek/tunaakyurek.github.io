Work / Actuated sonar-ring autonomy

Senior design project (EE491–492) · Boğaziçi University · Intelligent Systems Laboratory · Sep 2025 – Jun 2026

Mapping and exploring a maze with eight ultrasonic sensors

A Kobuki robot that explores an unknown maze, maps it and drives to a goal using only eight low-cost ultrasonic sensors on servo mounts. The same ROS 2 stack finished all ten simulated benchmark mazes and one complete mission on the physical robot.

Platform
Kobuki base carrying an actuated ring of eight HC-SR04 sensors; Gazebo simulation running the same ROS 2 stack
My role
Two-person project with Yakup Çalın, advised by Prof. H. Işıl Bozma. I did the initial MATLAB modelling, the ROS 2 implementation on the physical robot and the STM32H7 sensor-node design; the methodology and the mission development were shared.
Key results
  • 42 / 43 navigation goals reached in the full mission on the physical robot
  • 10 / 10 simulated benchmark mazes finished
  • 12 / 12 repeat runs on the four hardest mazes finished
Evidence
hardware · simulation — the hardware result is a single complete mission, not a repeated benchmark.

The problem

An ultrasonic ranger costs a few dollars and works in any lighting, but it returns a single distance over a cone about 60° wide: it tells you that something is there, not exactly where. We wanted to know whether a robot can explore and map an unknown maze with nothing better — no LiDAR, no camera, and no external positioning. The robot localises with wheel odometry and an IMU only[8].

What we built

  • Sensing. Eight HC-SR04 sensors on servos that sweep 45–135°, driven by two Arduino Mega + RAMPS boards that fire in a fixed order with a 30 ms guard so neighbouring sensors do not hear each other’s echoes[7].
  • Mapping. Each reading updates the whole beam cone, not a single ray, in a persistent 3-D voxel map; a confidence-gated 2-D projection of it feeds the planner.
  • Mission loop. The robot drives, stops, sweeps, updates the map and picks the next frontier. Simulation and hardware share the same ROS 2 interfaces, so one stack runs in both.

My own focus was the initial MATLAB model of the sensing and mapping, the ROS 2 implementation on the physical Kobuki, and the STM32H7 sensor-node design; the rest was shared work[11].

On the physical robot

In the cardboard maze the robot completed the full mission: it reached the goal after 4.27 m and 50 sensor sweeps, reaching 42 of 43 navigation goals with no aborted goals or recovery manoeuvres[3]. The map captures the layout and the free corridors, but walls come out thick and incomplete: against the hand-measured layout, occupied-cell IoU was 0.151 and 48.3 % of the area was covered[4]. It is good enough to explore and navigate, not to measure the room.

In simulation

Ten 8.6 m × 8.6 m mazes with a fixed start and goal; a run counts only if the robot reports FINISHED within 0.30 m of the goal. All ten finished[1], and repeating the four hardest mazes three times each gave 12 of 12[2]. Figures 4 and 5 are the easiest and the hardest of the report’s four examples[14].

Limitations

The hardware evidence is one complete mission, not a repeated benchmark. Localisation is odometry-only, so drift grows with distance, and the simulated maps share the hardware’s weakness on wall accuracy (IoU 0.07–0.15)[10]. A dynamic-obstacle layer exists but was off in every reported run[5].

Outputs

We presented the work as this poster[9] and documented it in the EE491–492 final report (June 2026; PDF on Google Drive). Separately, the Boğaziçi University Electrical & Electronics Engineering Department gave the project an Honorable Mention among its 2026 senior design projects[12].

Why the sensors sweep

A fixed sonar cone is precise about range but sees only part of a surface. In a controlled test with a 0.58 m panel at 0.60 m, one fixed sensor marked only cells that were really on the panel face but found about half of it; three swept sensors found all of it but also painted phantom wall to either side. Range bias stayed small, −0.040 to −0.049 m, in every configuration[15]. Coverage and lateral precision trade against each other; range is the reliable part.

System architecture and sensor model

For a sensor at pose x reporting range r, voxels inside the cone fall into a free region (range < r − δ), an occupied shell (|range − r| ≤ δ) and an unobserved region beyond. Free and occupied cells receive log-odds increments; in parallel, Beta evidence counts (α, β) separate “unknown because unobserved” from “unknown because the readings conflict”. A voxel locks as wall only when the Beta mean and the evidence count pass a confidence threshold, and repeated free-space evidence can reopen it. Simulation updates the full 30° half-angle cone; the hardware uses a more conservative 1° beam-axis model and a decaying navigation map.

Embedded design and beam-coverage analysis

I designed a custom STM32H7 ultrasonic node for lower-latency 40 kHz acquisition with envelope-based echo processing. It was assembled and probed on the bench but never ran on the robot; every result above uses the Arduino-driven HC-SR04 ring[6].
