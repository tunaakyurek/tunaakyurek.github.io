Distributed Formation–Containment Control and Adaptive Consensus Target Tracking in Multi-Agent Systems

Independent MATLAB reproductions and diagnostic ablations of two decentralized multi-agent architectures: adaptive formation–containment control for heterogeneous linear swarms and cross-covariance-aware dynamic-cluster target tracking across a 1,000-node sensor network.

Platform
MATLAB simulations of 11-agent heterogeneous leader–follower swarms and 1,000-node wireless sensor networks
Role
Individual graduate studies: closed-loop state-space implementation from published control/estimation equations, coupling-gain and Monte Carlo robustness ablations, and multi-round consensus contraction analysis.
Key results
  • ≈10⁻⁶ in ≈2 s leader formation and follower containment error norm convergence (Study 1)
  • 2.48 → 2.06 m target-tracking RMSE reduction from cross-covariance-aware cluster-head fusion (Study 2)
  • 3.94 vs 6.39 mean error-covariance trace tr(P) under correlated fusion vs. local adaptive consensus (lower on 100 % of steps)
Evaluation modality
simulation — independent MATLAB reproductions and diagnostic ablations of Jiang et al. (IEEE TAC) and Zhang et al. (IEEE TCYB).

Study 1 — Fully distributed formation–containment control

Reference: W. Jiang, G. Wen, Z. Peng, T. Huang, and A. Rahmani, “Fully Distributed Formation-Containment Control of Heterogeneous Linear Multiagent Systems,” IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control[1].

In an 11-node directed communication topology (Figure 3), six 6-state leaders—of which only leaders 1 and 2 receive the virtual leader r₀ directly—acquire a time-varying rotating hexagonal formation while driving four heterogeneous 4-state followers into the dynamic convex hull spanned by the leaders (Figure 1). Rather than relying on centralized knowledge of the communication graph’s algebraic connectivity λ₂, each agent updates its local coupling weight adaptively from relative state errors (Figure 7). Under the distributed adaptive observer and output-regulation law, both the leader formation-tracking error norm ‖ξl‖ and the follower containment error norm ‖ef‖ decay exponentially to the ≈ 10⁻⁶ numerical floor within ~2 s across a 10 s horizon (1.85×10⁻⁶ and 1.48×10⁻⁶) and across 20-seed Monte Carlo initial-condition sweeps (Figures 2 & 8)[1].

Study 2 — Adaptive consensus target tracking with dynamic clustering

Reference: H. Zhang, X. Zhou, Z. Wang, H. Yan, and J. Sun, “Adaptive Consensus-Based Distributed Target Tracking With Dynamic Cluster in Sensor Networks,” IEEE Transactions on Cybernetics[2].

Across a 1,000-node wireless sensor network (10 cluster heads and 990 regular sensor nodes; evaluated on a 100-sensor active cluster over K = 50 steps), event-triggered dynamic clusters activate around a manoeuvring target while sensor nodes exchange local state estimates and explicitly propagate inter-sensor cross-covariance matrices Pi,j (Figure 5)[2].

  • Cross-covariance-aware cluster-head fusion: Incorporating the maintained inter-sensor cross-covariances Pi,j at the cluster head (ACDF + correlated fusion) yields a fused covariance trace tr(P₀) strictly below the local sensor trace at 100 % of time steps (3.935 vs. 6.389 mean tr P), verifying the theoretical covariance bound P₀ ⪯ Pi (Lemma 2) and reducing target position RMSE from 2.484 m to 2.064 m (Figures 4 & 6)[2][4].
  • Diagnostic finding — single-step closed-loop tracking vs. multi-round consensus contraction: In the nominal single-step closed-loop simulation (L = 1 consensus pass per measurement step k), fresh measurement noise injected at every time step kept the Average Disagreement of Estimates (ADoE) at O(1–4) above the stopping threshold β = 0.1 (mean 1.44, min 0.256), so adaptive consensus weights (ACDF, 2.484 m RMSE) did not improve single-step tracking RMSE over fixed-gain consensus (CDF, 2.466 m RMSE)[2][3]. Isolating the multi-round consensus loop (L = 1…60 rounds without new measurement noise injection; Figure 9) verified that ADoE contracts exponentially below β = 0.1 within 15–25 consensus rounds (3.52 → 0.0085 by round 60)[3].

Limitations & scope

  • Literature reproduction and simulation scope: Both studies are independent MATLAB implementations and diagnostic ablations of the control and estimation architectures introduced by Jiang et al. (IEEE TAC) and Zhang et al. (IEEE TCYB); all experiments were conducted in numerical simulation[1][2].
